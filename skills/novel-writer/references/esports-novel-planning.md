# Esports Novel Planning Notes — 电竞小说规划参考

Use when planning or generating esports fiction, especially MOBA/campus-to-pro settings.

## Core planning principles

- Treat esports as both **competition** and **coming-of-age**: matches should advance character, relationships, roster status, and career stakes, not just produce wins.
- Avoid making a five-person lineup permanent by default. For career-spanning stories, prefer **dynamic roster / esports career ensemble**:
  - campus team / school club
  - college league lineup
  - youth training or tryout group
  - academy / secondary league team
  - pro roster with trades, substitutions, retirements, and role conflicts
- Each important teammate should have a position, strength, shortcoming, decision point, and later destination. Some can leave, become rivals, or reunite.

## Protagonist strength without flattening tension

A protagonist may be both mechanically gifted and a high-level shot-caller, but avoid "operation top + shot-calling top + always right".

Recommended framing:
- **Early strength**: top mechanical talent + instinctive map/tempo reading.
- **Early limitation**: wild/grassroots shot-calling, not yet professionalized.
- **Growth line**: from "wild genius caller" to "professional team brain".

Useful constraints:
- sees opportunities faster than teammates can execute
- gives commands too quickly or unclearly
- lacks professional vocabulary for wave states, vision layers, resource windows, tempo, and risk tradeoffs
- trusts personal judgment more than team preparation
- struggles with meta adaptation, hero pool targeting, stamina, pressure, or teammate emotions

This preserves power fantasy while giving real growth space.

## MOBA match-writing checklist

Key matches should have concrete win/loss reasons. Use several, not all, per match:
- draft/BP plan and counter-plan
- lane priority and matchup pressure
- jungle pathing, invade, countergank, or vision denial
- wave state, reset timing, lane swap, resource trade
- objective setup: dragon/baron/ancient resource, fake start, turn, or steal
- split push / 4-1 / 1-3-1 pressure
- teamfight target priority and cooldown tracking
- shot-call clarity and execution gap
- psychological bait, feint, or long setup paid off later

High-fire match payoff should not only be "the protagonist outplays". Stronger payoffs include:
- the team finally executes a call everyone used to miss
- a plan planted several minutes earlier becomes visible on the map
- a weaker teammate wins a small but decisive task
- the protagonist chooses restraint instead of forcing the hero play

## Tone and trope guardrails

For youth/campus esports with high-fire matches:
- Use light comedy in dorms, cafeteria, training room, club recruitment, and review banter.
- Reduce jokes during elimination, major losses, departures, confessions, or career decisions.
- Face-slapping can be a stage reward, not the main engine. Avoid repeated "mocked → wins → everyone shocked" loops.
- Opponents should have coherent goals and preparation; do not make formal youth training/pro teams stupid just to praise the grassroots protagonist.
- Do not overuse commercial darkness, fan wars, or cyberbullying unless the project explicitly wants that tone.

## Length guidance

For a campus-to-pro esports novel:
- 12–18 chapters: campus tournament only.
- 30–45 chapters: campus/high-school or college league through tryout.
- 65–80 chapters at ~3k words/chapter: about 20–24万字, enough for campus start → college league → youth training → first pro-stage proof.
- 100+ chapters: full multi-season career, trades, domestic league, international stage.

When the user wants dynamic rosters and career growth, recommend around **20–25万字 / 65–80 chapters** as a stable first-book scope, with sequel room for top league/world championship.
