# Staged Long-Novel Planning — 全书主线 + 分卷精规划

Use this when a long-form novel has multiple arcs/volumes, dynamic casts, competitions, journeys, school/career stages, or evolving teams. The goal is to avoid both under-planning and over-planning.

## Core principle

Do **not** fully pre-plan every faction, team, match, transfer, clue, and side character for the entire book before chapter generation.

Instead use:

1. **Global spine lock** — stable whole-book direction.
2. **Small long-foreshadowing ledger** — only a few full-book callbacks.
3. **Current-volume precision planning** — detailed characters, opponents, chapter briefs, and short foreshadowing for the next volume only.
4. **Post-volume recalibration** — after completing a volume, refine the next volume using what actually worked in prose.

This is especially important when the cast is dynamic: teammates, rivals, schools, clubs, factions, or organizations may change by stage.

## What to lock globally

Lock these early:

- title / working title
- genre and reader promise
- main style, supporting styles, forbidden styles
- core element mix and forbidden elements
- target length range and chapter scale
- whole-book protagonist growth line
- major volume structure and end target
- stable world/system rules needed from chapter 1
- a few class-level constraints, e.g. “dynamic roster, not fixed five-person team”

Keep global planning compact enough to remain usable.

## What not to over-plan globally

Do not fully detail these for the whole book unless the user explicitly wants a full master bible:

- every team/faction roster
- every tournament bracket or match
- every transfer/trade
- every minor opponent
- every side character background
- every version/rule change
- every short clue or payoff
- every battle/match BP and tactical sequence

Use placeholders at the global level, then detail them when the relevant volume starts.

## Long foreshadowing ledger

At global-planning stage, plan only **3–6 long foreshadowing threads**.

Good long foreshadowing threads:
- are tied to protagonist growth, theme, rivalry, dynamic cast, or final payoff
- can be seeded in volume 1 and echoed across later volumes
- should not require every chapter to service them
- are tracked in a ledger with: first seed, mid echoes, final payoff, theme served, pitfalls

Short foreshadowing belongs in volume/chapter briefs, not the global bible.

## Volume precision planning

Before drafting a volume, create a volume bible/brief that includes:

- volume goal and endpoint
- chapter count and rough word budget
- active cast and temporary cast
- current teams/factions/opponents
- local rules/locations/events
- key competitions/battles/cases/arcs
- long-foreshadowing touchpoints in this volume
- short foreshadowing local to this volume
- detailed chapter briefs for the volume

After the volume is completed, inspect what relationships and plot devices worked before planning the next volume in detail.

## Competition / tournament / match-density planning

For sports/esports/tournament stories, calculate match density before finalizing length.

Classify competitions:

- **Full key match / battle / case**: 3–6 chapters. Use for volume finals, rival clashes, promotion/relegation, major growth proof.
- **Medium important match**: 1.5–2.5 chapters. Use for strong opponents and stage validation.
- **Short showcase**: 0.5–1 chapter. Use to show a tactic, weakness, or character beat.
- **Summarized record**: a few paragraphs to half a chapter. Use for routine bracket/group-stage wins/losses.

Then estimate chapters per stage before committing to total length. If the calculated count exceeds the target, either expand the book or reduce the number of fully dramatized matches.

## Escalation realism

Do not let high-level institutions notice the protagonist too early unless the premise demands it.

Example for esports/sports:
- School/internal stage: local recognition only.
- City/regional stage: broader school-circle recognition.
- National stage: scouts/academy/professional observers can reasonably appear.
- Professional stage: contracts, rosters, substitutes, transfers, and staff pressure become central.

This prevents a small early win from feeling like an unrealistic leap to national/professional attention.

## Pitfalls

- **Over-planning trap**: designing all future rosters/teams before writing volume 1, causing stale or unused canon.
- **Under-planning trap**: starting chapter prose with no global growth line or final-stage target.
- **Mechanical-failure trap**: forcing “one big failure per volume.” Prefer “real resistance and growth cost,” which may be a loss, roster change, exposed tactic, trust crack, opportunity tradeoff, or hard-won victory.
- **Premature-scouting trap**: scouts/professional attention appear too early after local wins.
- **Match-rush trap**: too many tournaments inside one volume, leaving no room for character consequences.
